SECTION 01

How to use this guide

Read it twice. Highlight what surprises you. Practice the model answers aloud.

The principle

You are not preparing to recite. You are preparing to think out loud with the same fluency you'd use describing your favorite movie. Every concept here is paired with a model answer. Read it. Internalize the structure. Then forget the words and keep the structure.

The 3-pass method

  1. Pass 1 — read end-to-end. Just understanding. No notes, no highlighting. ~2 hours.
  2. Pass 2 — highlight + annotate. Mark the answers you struggled with. Re-read sections where the concept feels weak. ~2 hours.
  3. Pass 3 — speak aloud. Pick 20 questions a day for a week. Speak the answer out loud, as if to a CISO. This is where memory becomes muscle. ~30 min/day for 7 days.
Mental anchor: If you can answer a question in 2 minutes, with one strong example, in language a non-technical board member could follow — you've nailed it. That's the bar.

Three universal rules

  • 1. Always answer in three parts. Define → Example → Implication. Even short questions deserve this structure.
  • 2. Use one number per answer. Numbers anchor credibility. "We reduced audit effort by 55%" beats "we reduced it a lot."
  • 3. Never say "It depends" without then explaining. Always follow with: "It depends on X. If X is A then Y. If X is B then Z."

SECTION 03

Core concept explanations

Every concept you'll be asked to explain — with the structure to think through it on the fly.

What is GRC?

GRC stands for Governance, Risk, and Compliance. Three letters. Three jobs. One purpose: prove the organization is in control of itself.

Q: What's the difference between Governance, Risk, and Compliance?
Governance = who is allowed to decide what. The authority structure.
Risk = what could go wrong and how much of that we'll accept.
Compliance = proving we meet the rules — law, regulation, contract.
Each one is a distinct discipline. Most people conflate them, and that's the root of most GRC failures.
Q: Difference between compliance and assurance?
Compliance is the state of meeting an obligation. Assurance is the activity of proving it with defensible evidence. A firm can be compliant without being assured (they meet the rules but can't prove it), and assured without being secure (great paperwork but a real attacker still breaks in). Mature firms are all three.
Q: Walk me through the Three Lines of Defense.
1st Line — Own It: business operations and IT teams; they take risks to make money, run controls every day, attest to their effectiveness.
2nd Line — Challenge It: CISO function, GRC team, compliance, operational risk; sets policy, challenges 1st line decisions, reports to executive and board.
3rd Line — Verify It: Internal Audit; reports to Audit Committee, independent of management, provides assurance to the board.
These three lines are designed to not trust each other. When they all agree too easily, something is wrong.
Memory anchor: "OWN — CHALLENGE — VERIFY". 3 lines, 3 verbs. Never forget the order.

Risk Appetite vs. Risk Tolerance vs. Risk Capacity

This is one of the highest-value concepts in GRC. Imagine three concentric circles:

  • Risk Capacity (outer) = the wall. The maximum risk the firm can absorb without threatening solvency, license, or strategy. A function of the balance sheet.
  • Risk Appetite (middle) = the speed limit. Board-approved level of risk the firm will accept in pursuit of strategy. Stated quantitatively where possible.
  • Risk Tolerance (inner) = the cruising lane. The variation around appetite that's acceptable in execution. Operationalised into KRIs and limits.

Most enterprises confuse all three. That alone explains half of governance failures. As a GRC consultant, the first thing you do is force the client to define them with numbers.

Q: What's the difference between inherent and residual risk?
Inherent risk = how risky something is before any controls are applied.
Residual risk = how risky it remains after the controls do their job.
The space between them is what your control investment is buying. Residual risk above appetite triggers management action. Residual risk above capacity is an existential issue.

The four risk treatment choices

  • Mitigate — add controls to reduce likelihood or impact.
  • Transfer — shift financial impact to a third party (cyber insurance, contractual indemnity).
  • Avoid — stop the activity that creates the risk.
  • Accept — knowingly retain the risk because controls cost more than the risk justifies. Must be documented, signed, dated, and reviewed.
Memory anchor: "M-T-A-A" — Mitigate, Transfer, Avoid, Accept. Some people say "the 4 R's" — Reduce, Reassign, Refuse, Retain. Same thing.
Q: What's the most-abused word in GRC?
"Accept." Risk acceptance is a valid choice — but only if it's documented, approved by the right authority, time-bound, and reviewed. "We accept this risk" said in a meeting and never written down is not an acceptance — it's negligence with a label.

The PDCA cycle — the heartbeat of every ISO standard

  • Plan — context, scope, risks, objectives, policies.
  • Do — implement controls, run operations.
  • Check — monitor, measure, audit, review.
  • Act — fix gaps, improve, adapt.

It's the operating system. Every ISMS task fits in one of these four boxes. Every annual cycle is one full rotation.

Q: What is "Continuous Control Monitoring" (CCM) and how does it differ from traditional periodic compliance?
Traditional compliance is periodic — quarterly or annual evidence collection, snapshot testing, point-in-time attestations.
CCM instruments controls so they emit real-time signals — pass, fail, exception. A platform aggregates the signals, computes control health, identifies drift, and generates audit-ready evidence on demand. The state of compliance becomes continuous, not periodic.
For an auditor it's the difference between "show me a screenshot from March 12" and "give me read-only access to the platform and I'll see everything from the audit period myself."

Likely questions

Q: Walk me through how you designed the Common Control Framework.
Step 1 — Obligations register. Listed every regulation, framework, and contractual obligation the client was subject to. That gave us the universe of "what must be true."
Step 2 — Decompose. Broke each framework into specific control requirements — SOC 2 has 64 criteria across 5 TSC categories; ISO 27001 has 93 controls in 4 themes. Total atomic requirements: ~700.
Step 3 — Cluster. Grouped requirements that ask the same fundamental thing — e.g., "MFA on privileged access" appears in SOC 2 CC6.1, ISO 27001 A.8.5, PCI DSS 8.4, HIPAA §164.312(d). One operational control, four citations.
Step 4 — Consolidate. Designed unified controls satisfying each cluster. Got from ~700 requirements down to 400 unified controls.
Step 5 — Map. Built bidirectional mapping — control-to-citation and citation-to-control. Maintained in a control library tool.
Step 6 — Govern. Single accountable owner per unified control, change-controlled, regulator-update process.
Result: Test once. Attest many. That's the architectural win — and where the $2.1M savings came from.
Q: How exactly did you automate evidence collection?
For AWS — we used Security Hub as the aggregator, Config rules for continuous configuration checks, and CloudTrail for activity evidence. Wrote Python collectors that on a schedule pull findings, normalize them against the CCF control IDs, and dump to a centralized evidence lake.
For Azure — Defender for Cloud findings + Azure Policy compliance + Azure Activity Logs, similar collector pattern.
For GCP — Security Command Centre + Organization Policy + Cloud Audit Logs.
Above the cloud collectors we layered identity (Okta), endpoint (CrowdStrike), and SIEM (Splunk). Every collected artifact was tagged with: source system, timestamp, control ID, integrity hash. Stored in S3 with object-lock for immutability.
Q: How did you handle 1,200 misconfigurations? That's a lot.
Two passes. Pass 1 — triage by exploitability. Worked with the engineering and SOC teams to classify each misconfig: internet-exposed? privileged data path? active attack surface? That gave us 180 "fix this week" items.
Pass 2 — automation. About 65% of the remaining misconfigs were repeat patterns — public S3 buckets, MFA gaps, missing encryption, over-permissioned IAM. We wrote Terraform modules to enforce secure defaults and OPA policies to prevent recurrence. Then sequenced remediation with the cloud-engineering team across 14 weeks. Drift detection via Prometheus and Splunk caught anything that regressed.
Q: What was the hardest part of the project?
Honestly — convincing the client's audit team to trust automated evidence. They had 8 years of habit collecting screenshots manually. The CCF + automation pitch was technically sound but culturally threatening. We solved it by running both processes in parallel for the first audit cycle — let the auditors compare manual evidence to automated evidence on the same controls, and the automated proved to be both more complete and more defensible. After that they were converts.
Q: How did you measure $2.1M in savings?
Built a defensible model. Took the client's previous-cycle audit effort — external audit fees + internal compliance team hours + engineering evidence-collection time. Multiplied by 5 frameworks. Applied a 55% effort-reduction observed in the new state. Validated with the CFO's office. Conservative — didn't include risk-of-finding reduction, audit-finding-remediation reduction, or executive-time savings. Actual all-in benefit was higher.

  • The reporting. Quarterly Compliance Programme reviews — exec-facing.

The 60-second version

"I led NIST CSF-based maturity assessments for two very different clients — a Tier-2 commercial bank in Nepal preparing for first-time SIEM and IR capability, and a mid-size US pharma preparing for FDA 21 CFR Part 11 cybersecurity compliance. Each was a 6-week engagement: structured interviews against all 108 CSF subcategories, sample testing, current-state maturity rating, gap classification, and a 3-year transformation roadmap. Pharma engagement produced a $4.2M investment plan with 47 prioritized gaps; both roadmaps were adopted by their boards as formal cybersecurity programmes."

Q: Walk me through your NIST CSF assessment methodology.
Phase 1 — Mobilization (Week 1). Scope, charter, stakeholder map, document request list.
Phase 2 — Discovery (Weeks 2-3). ~25 interviews across IT, security, business, audit, legal. Document review. Sample testing of high-priority controls.
Phase 3 — Maturity scoring (Week 4). Each of 108 CSF subcategories scored on a 5-level scale — Initial, Developing, Defined, Managed, Optimized. Backed by at least two evidence types per score.
Phase 4 — Gap analysis & roadmap (Week 5). Gaps classified P1-P4 by risk and feasibility. Roadmap built — quick wins, structural fixes, long-horizon investments.
Phase 5 — Reporting (Week 6). Executive summary, full report, board presentation. Heatmap by function and by domain.
Output: maturity verdict, top 3 risks, prioritised remediation plan, and indicative investment range.
Q: How do you make NIST CSF maturity ratings defensible?
Three rules. One: evidence-based, never opinion-only. Every score backed by ≥2 evidence types — document + observation, document + interview corroboration, etc.
Two: distinction between "indicator of existence" and "indicator of effectiveness." A policy that exists but isn't followed scores Level 2 at best — not Level 3.
Three: triangulation across first/second/third line. If first line says "this control works" but third line found gaps last year, the score lives in the gap.
Q: The bank scored 1.8/5. Was that disappointing?
Actually no — it was the right starting point. Maturity scoring isn't a grade; it's a baseline. The board needed an honest picture. 1.8 told them they were at "Developing" — capability existed in pockets but no consistent firm-wide practice. From there we built a 3-year plan to bring them to 3.5 — a defensible "Managed" state. The board adopted the roadmap because the diagnostic was honest, not flattering.
Q: How is NIST CSF 2.0 different from 1.1?
Two big changes. Govern became a 6th function (added to Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) — formally bringing governance into the framework. Reflects regulator pressure for accountable cyber governance.
Second, broader applicability beyond critical infrastructure — explicitly aimed at SMEs, supply chain, sector-agnostic.
Third — though smaller — clearer integration with other frameworks (mapped to ISO 27001, COBIT, FAIR) for harmonised use.
Q: For pharma, how does 21 CFR Part 11 differ from typical SOC 2?
21 CFR Part 11 is FDA's regulation for electronic records and electronic signatures used in FDA-regulated processes. Three core focus areas: electronic records integrity, electronic signature equivalence to handwritten, and audit trails. Where SOC 2 is broad availability/security/processing-integrity for service organisations, Part 11 is narrow but deeper on the records and signatures themselves — system validation, role-based access, audit trail completeness, and protection from alteration. A pharma typically needs both — SOC 2 for general assurance and Part 11 for FDA-regulated workflows.

  • Methodology design — the 6-week framework you reused 4 times.
  • Workshop facilitation across 25 interviews per engagement.
  • Board presentations — translating tech gaps to financial risk.
  • ISO 27001 policy suite design for the Nepal client.

Q: How does DPDP 2023 compare to GDPR?
Substantial structural overlap — both define data principals/subjects, fiduciaries/controllers, consent as the primary processing ground, breach notification, individual rights.
Key differences: (1) DPDP uses "Data Fiduciary" terminology (vs GDPR's "Controller") with a unique Significant Data Fiduciary tier for high-impact entities. (2) DPDP's penalties are capped at ₹250 crores per breach (vs GDPR's 4% of global turnover — generally larger). (3) DPDP cross-border transfer is via a positive list ("blacklist except where notified") — more permissive than GDPR's adequacy requirement. (4) Consent withdrawal is mandatory and equally easy as giving — sharp focus. (5) Children's data — stricter ban on tracking and behavioral monitoring of minors.
Q: What does DPDP mean for enterprises operationally?
Five operational shifts. One — consent architecture: must collect, manage, withdraw, and audit consent. Needs consent management platform.
Two — Data Principal rights: access, correction, erasure, grievance redressal — operationalised within prescribed timelines.
Three — Data Fiduciary obligations: appointed DPO for Significant Data Fiduciaries, DPIA where required, breach notification within prescribed period.
Four — Processor due-diligence: contracts updated with DPDP-compliant clauses; vendor TPRM extended to cover DPDP fitness.
Five — Cross-border transfers: monitor the Government's positive list; have fallback in place.
Q: How did you embed AI governance into the DPDP advisory?
DPDP touches AI in three places. One — automated decision-making: where significant decisions about individuals are made by algorithms, there must be transparency, human review pathways, and right of redress. We mapped this to EU AI Act Article 14 (human oversight) and NIST AI RMF Manage function.
Two — children's data: tracking and behavioral monitoring barred — has direct implications for AI personalization systems. We drafted enterprise guidance on legitimate vs prohibited use.
Three — risk tiering: we proposed an AI risk-tiering aligned with EU AI Act (Prohibited / High-risk / Limited / Minimal) embedded into the Significant Data Fiduciary concept.
The integration was the differentiator — DPDP became future-ready for AI without needing a separate Act.

Q: What are the four types of ITGC controls?
Four classic categories — Change Management (testing, approval, segregation between development and production); Access Management (provisioning, periodic review, privileged access, deprovisioning); Computer Operations (backups, batch processing, incident management); Program Development (SDLC, testing standards, deployment). Some frameworks add a 5th — Logical Security covering passwords, MFA, session controls.
Q: What are the five SOC 2 Trust Service Criteria?
Security (the only mandatory one — Common Criteria), Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, Privacy. Each client picks which ones apply — almost everyone takes Security; SaaS providers usually take Security + Availability + Confidentiality. Privacy is the most rigorous addition.
Q: SOC 2 Type I vs Type II — what's the difference?
Type I = design of controls at a point in time. Auditor confirms the controls are well-designed. Faster, cheaper, less defensible.
Type II = operating effectiveness over a period (typically 6-12 months). Auditor tests samples and confirms the controls actually operated as designed. Slower, more rigorous, what enterprises actually want from their vendors.
Q: How did you get 3 clients to clean SOC 2 Type II on first attempt?
Three things consistently. One — pre-audit gap assessment 6 months out. We knew where the gaps were before the auditor did. Two — evidence calendar: every control had an evidence-collection cadence aligned to the audit period, so when fieldwork started, we had 12 months of contemporaneous evidence — not retrofitted.
Three — control owner training. Every control had a named human accountable; we ran 90-minute sessions teaching them what to expect, how to answer auditor questions, and how to push back appropriately. The classic killer of audits — owners surprised in the room — never happened.
Q: What's a CUEC?
Complementary User Entity Control. A control that the user of the service has to operate for the service provider's controls to function as designed. Example — a SaaS provider's SOC 2 might assume customers (user entities) provision and deprovision their own user accounts. That's a CUEC. The auditor lists them in the SOC report so customers know what controls THEY need to operate on their side. Missing or vague CUECs are a common audit finding.

Q: What are the four validation layers?
Layer 1 — Format. Schema validation per evidence type. A SOC 2 access-review evidence has a different schema than a backup-completion log. Each control's evidence has a defined schema; artifacts that don't conform are rejected.
Layer 2 — Freshness. Each control has a required collection window — e.g., quarterly access reviews must be within the last 90 days. Stale evidence is flagged.
Layer 3 — Completeness. Required fields present — for an access review, did we get user list, reviewer signature, approval date, exception handling? Missing fields trigger flags.
Layer 4 — Authority. Was the evidence signed/approved by the right role? An access review signed by a junior analyst when policy requires a manager signature = invalid.
Q: How does the AI match scoring work?
Two-stage. First — we embed both the control assertion ("MFA is enforced on all privileged accounts at all times") and the evidence ("Okta admin report showing MFA enrolment for users in 'Admins' group as of 2025-Q3"). Cosine similarity on Titan embeddings gives us a first-pass relevance score.
Second — we use Claude on Bedrock to evaluate semantic match: does this evidence actually prove the assertion? It returns a score 0-100 with reasoning. Below 70 — flagged for human review. Below 50 — rejected automatically with reasoning sent back to the evidence owner.
Q: How does the retrofitted-evidence detector work?
Retrofitting is the cardinal sin of audit. Auditors hate it because it's fraud. Our detector flags suspicious patterns in file metadata: (1) creation timestamps clustered within hours of one another for evidence supposedly spanning months. (2) identical formatting across artifacts that should have different sources. (3) file metadata showing the artifact was created AFTER the date it's claiming to evidence. (4) uniform language patterns across artifacts supposedly authored by different people.
Each artifact gets a "retrofit risk score." High scores → flagged for compliance officer review before the auditor sees them. Saved the client from a Major NC in one cycle.
Q: How did you measure 92% validation accuracy?
Ground-truth comparison. We had two human reviewers (one compliance officer, one external auditor) classify 500 evidence artifacts as Pass / Fail / Investigate. Then the AEV classified the same 500 independently. Accuracy = (AEV agrees with majority human verdict) ÷ total. 92% agreement. The 8% disagreement was almost entirely on edge cases where humans themselves disagreed.

Q: What is MCP and why use it for control verification?

For control verification, MCP gave us a clean abstraction: each cloud or security tool gets an MCP server exposing standard verification queries. Instead of bespoke integrations per tool, we have one orchestrator that talks to many MCP servers — easy to extend, version-controlled, audit-defensible.
We also use LLMs to compose multi-tool queries — e.g., "verify MFA on AWS admins" requires the AWS MCP server (for IAM users) AND the Okta MCP server (for MFA enrolment) AND the SIEM MCP server (for recent admin logins without MFA). One natural-language verification request gets decomposed into three MCP calls automatically.
Q: Give me an example — how do you verify "MFA on privileged accounts"?
Five atomic checks across three MCP servers:
Check 1 (Okta MCP): Pull all users in the "Privileged" group. Get count.
Check 2 (Okta MCP): For each user, check MFA factor enrolment. Verify ≥2 factors.
Check 3 (Okta MCP): Pull MFA exception list. Verify each exception has documented business justification + expiry < 90 days.
Check 4 (Splunk MCP): Query last 30 days of admin logins. Verify each used MFA — i.e., the auth event log shows factor verification.
Check 5 (AWS MCP): Cross-check that IAM users with admin policies match the Okta privileged group — flag drift.
Aggregate the 5 checks → if all pass, control is GREEN. Any fail → control is RED. Partial → AMBER. Verdict pushed to GRC tool with evidence bundle.

The foundational vocabulary — what every ERM programme must define first

Q: Define Risk Appetite, Risk Capacity, Risk Threshold, and Risk Tolerance — and explain how they relate.
Think of these as four concentric boundaries — outer to inner.

Risk Capacity — the absolute outer wall. The maximum risk the organisation can absorb without threatening its solvency, regulatory license, or strategic existence. Determined by financial strength, capital buffers, and legal constraints. This is not chosen — it's calculated. Example: "Our capital reserves can absorb a £50M unplanned loss without triggering regulatory capital inadequacy."

Risk Appetite — the board-set speed limit inside capacity. The type and quantum of risk the organisation is willing to accept in pursuit of its strategic objectives. It must be board-approved, strategy-linked, and expressed both qualitatively (a statement) and quantitatively (metric thresholds). Example: "We are willing to accept moderate operational risk in pursuit of our international expansion, but we have zero appetite for regulatory breach or data loss." This is the anchor of the entire ERM programme.

Risk Tolerance — the operational bandwidth around appetite. The acceptable variation in risk exposure before management escalates. If appetite is the speed limit, tolerance is the variance on either side that doesn't trigger an alert. Example: "Our credit loss appetite is 2% of loan book. Tolerance is ±0.4% — so 2.4% triggers a management review."

Risk Threshold — the hard-stop trigger point. The level at which a risk exposure must be escalated to the board or a formal treatment decision must be made. Thresholds operationalise appetite. Example: "If residual risk on any Category 1 risk exceeds 'High' on our 5×5 matrix for two consecutive quarters, it is automatically escalated to the Risk Committee."

The relationship: Capacity > Appetite > Tolerance > Threshold. Appetite is aspirational; threshold is operational. Most ERM programmes define appetite but never operationalise it into thresholds — that gap is where programmes collapse.
Q: Walk me through how you built the Risk Appetite Statement for this client.
Six steps.
Step 1 — Strategy anchoring. Pulled the CFO and CEO into a 2-hour working session. Mapped the firm's three-year strategic objectives — revenue growth in 3 new markets, IPO readiness, product expansion. Asked: "For each objective, what risks are you willing to take? What are you never willing to risk?"
Step 2 — Risk taxonomy alignment. Organised appetite by the seven risk categories in our taxonomy: Operational, Cyber, Credit/Financial, Regulatory/Compliance, Reputational, Strategic, Third-Party. Drafted a qualitative appetite statement for each category — from "zero tolerance" (regulatory breach) to "moderate appetite" (market expansion risk).
Step 3 — Quantification. Converted qualitative statements into metrics. For cyber: maximum tolerable unplanned downtime = 4 hours/year; maximum tolerable data records compromised = zero. For financial: maximum tolerable credit loss variance = ±0.4% above stated loss rate. Each metric became a KRI.
Step 4 — Threshold setting. For each KRI, defined three zones: Green (within appetite), Amber (approaching threshold — management action), Red (threshold breached — board escalation).
Step 5 — Board validation. Presented the draft RAS to the Risk Committee. Two rounds of revision — they raised the tolerance on market risk (more growth-oriented than I initially modelled) and tightened the regulatory breach threshold.
Step 6 — Embed into governance. RAS published as a Board-approved policy. Linked to the KRI dashboard — live status visible to CRO monthly, Board quarterly.
Q: Walk me through the full risk management lifecycle — identification through monitoring.
I use a 5-stage cycle aligned to ISO 31000.

Stage 1 — Risk Identification. Surfaced risks through: structured workshops with 14 business function heads (using a pre-built facilitation pack — scenario cards, risk prompts by category), external horizon scanning (regulatory change, industry threat intelligence), and review of prior audit findings and incident logs. Output: raw risk universe of 140+ risks.

Stage 2 — Risk Analysis. Assessed each risk on two dimensions — likelihood (1-5 scale: Rare / Unlikely / Possible / Likely / Almost Certain) and impact (1-5 scale: Negligible / Minor / Moderate / Major / Catastrophic). Produced a 5×5 inherent risk heat map. Included velocity (how fast the risk materialises — important for cyber and reputational risks) and interconnectedness (risks that amplify each other). Top-20 risks identified.

Stage 3 — Risk Evaluation. Compared inherent risk score against the Risk Appetite Statement. Any risk scoring above the stated appetite threshold was flagged for mandatory treatment. Risks below appetite but above tolerance were flagged for monitoring. Risks within tolerance were accepted and logged.

— Mitigate: implement or enhance controls to reduce likelihood or impact.
— Transfer: use insurance, contractual indemnity, or outsourcing to shift financial exposure.
— Avoid: exit the activity that creates the risk.
— Accept: formally document that the risk is knowingly retained — requires CRO sign-off and a time-bound review date.

Q: What's the difference between a risk register and a risk appetite statement? People confuse them.
They're complementary, not the same.
The Risk Register is the operational ledger — every identified risk, with its inherent score, current controls, residual score, owner, and treatment plan. It's a living document, updated quarterly. Think of it as the risk inventory.
The Risk Appetite Statement is the governing policy — the board's view of how much risk the organisation will accept by category, with quantitative thresholds. It's the benchmark against which the risk register is evaluated.
The relationship: the risk register identifies and measures risks; the RAS tells you whether those measured risks are acceptable. Without a RAS, the register is just a list — there's no way to answer "is this risk too high?"
Q: How did you design KRIs and what makes a good KRI vs a bad one?
KRI — Key Risk Indicator — is a metric that gives early warning that a risk is growing before it materialises into a loss event.

What makes a good KRI:
— Leading, not lagging. A KRI should signal before the event, not measure after it. "Number of failed change management approvals in the last 30 days" (leading) vs "number of production incidents caused by unauthorised changes" (lagging).
— Measurable and objective. A KRI must have a data source, an owner, and a collection frequency. "Management sentiment" is not a KRI.
— Threshold-linked. Every KRI must have a Green / Amber / Red threshold tied to the risk appetite. A number without a threshold is just data.
— Actionable. When a KRI turns Amber or Red, it must trigger a defined response — not just a note in a report.

Bad KRIs I've seen in practice:
— Too many: one client had 300 KRIs — impossible to monitor meaningfully. We cut to 45 high-signal ones.
— Lagging disguised as leading: "number of audit findings" is a lagging KRI.
— Unmeasured: "employee morale" with no collection mechanism.

For this client, I designed 45 KRIs across 7 risk categories. Each had: metric definition, data source, collection owner, frequency, and Green/Amber/Red thresholds. Reviewed with the CRO before publishing.

COSO ERM 2017 — more strategy-centric and governance-focused. Explicitly links ERM to corporate strategy and performance. Five components: Governance & Culture, Strategy & Objective-Setting, Performance, Review & Revision, Information & Reporting. Better for US-listed companies or those with a strong CFO/audit-committee governance frame.
In practice, for this engagement I used ISO 31000 as the process spine and COSO ERM for the governance and board reporting structure — the two are complementary.

What ISO 42001 is — and why it matters

Q: What is ISO 42001 and how does it differ from ISO 27001?
ISO 42001 — published in December 2023 — is the international standard for an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS). It provides a framework for organisations that develop, provide, or use AI systems to manage AI responsibly and systematically.

Like ISO 27001, it follows the PDCA-based management system structure (Clauses 4-10) and is certifiable. But where ISO 27001 is about protecting information from threats, ISO 42001 is about governing AI systems — ensuring they are developed and deployed responsibly, transparently, and with appropriate human oversight.

Key differences:
— ISO 27001 focuses on confidentiality, integrity, availability of information. ISO 42001 focuses on transparency, accountability, fairness, robustness, and human oversight of AI systems.
— ISO 27001 has 93 controls in Annex A. ISO 42001 has controls in Annex A covering AI policy, AI risk management, data governance for AI, AI system impact assessment, and AI system life cycle.
— ISO 27001 is mature (30+ years). ISO 42001 is new — organisations implementing it now are among the first movers globally.

They're complementary. An AI-powered product typically needs both: ISO 27001 for information security of the underlying system, ISO 42001 for governance of the AI decision-making itself.
Q: Walk me through how you built the AI Model Inventory Register.

Step 1 — Discovery. Ran a 3-week discovery exercise: surveyed all engineering teams, product teams, and data science leads. Also scanned the cloud infrastructure — SageMaker, Azure ML, Vertex AI — for deployed endpoints not captured in surveys. Found 12 "shadow" models deployed without formal registration.

Step 2 — Register fields. Each entry in the register captured:
— Model ID and name
— Business function and owner
— AI system type (generative, discriminative, recommendation, etc.)
— Purpose and use case description
— Data inputs — including personal data categories, sensitive data flags
— Output type — decision, recommendation, content generation, prediction
— Deployment status — development, staging, production, retired
— EU AI Act classification — Prohibited / High-Risk / Limited Risk / Minimal Risk
— ISO 42001 risk tier — Tier 1 (highest) to Tier 3
— Human oversight mechanism — fully automated / human-in-the-loop / human-on-the-loop / human-in-command
— Last impact assessment date
— Control implementation status

Step 3 — Validation. Each entry reviewed with the model owner and the data privacy team. Data processing fields cross-referenced with the GDPR RoPA (Record of Processing Activities) — any AI model processing personal data was linked to the RoPA entry.

Output: 63 registered AI/ML services. 8 classified as EU AI Act High-Risk. 12 shadow models now formally registered. Register stored in Confluence with quarterly refresh mandated.
Q: Walk me through your AI risk classification methodology.
I used a dual-axis classification: EU AI Act tiering as the regulatory lens, and ISO 42001 risk tiering as the internal operational lens.

EU AI Act tiering (4 levels):
— Prohibited: AI systems explicitly banned — social scoring by public authorities, exploitation of vulnerable groups, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces. None of our client's models fell here.
— High-Risk: AI systems in Annex III categories — employment decisions (CV screening), credit scoring, access to essential services, biometric identification, law enforcement. Our client had 8 high-risk models: an automated CV screening tool for internal HR use, an ML-based fraud detection model affecting account access, and a credit limit recommendation engine. Each required: conformity assessment, human oversight implementation, transparency documentation, and registration in the EU AI Act database (when operational).
— Limited Risk: AI systems that interact with humans — chatbots, LLM-powered features — requiring transparency obligations (users must know they're interacting with AI). 14 of our client's models fell here.
— Minimal Risk: Everything else — spam filters, recommendation engines, internal analytics models. 41 models. Lighter-touch governance applies.

ISO 42001 internal tiering (3 tiers):
Tier 1 (Highest): models making or directly influencing decisions that affect individuals' rights, financial outcomes, or employment. Full control suite required.
Tier 2 (Medium): models assisting human decision-making with significant business impact. Standard control suite required.
Tier 3 (Lower): internal tools, analytics, low-impact automation. Baseline controls required.

The scoring matrix assessed each model on: impact on individuals, reversibility of model output, degree of human oversight, data sensitivity, and regulatory applicability. Scores aggregated to Tier assignment. Any EU AI Act High-Risk model was automatically Tier 1 regardless of score.
Q: What controls did you implement under ISO 42001 and how did you structure them?
ISO 42001's Annex A organises controls across 9 domains. We implemented 38 controls across 7 active domains (2 were not applicable given the client's context).

Domain 1 — AI Policies (A.2): Established an AI Use Policy, an AI Ethics Policy, and an Acceptable Use Policy for generative AI tools. Board-approved. All staff trained within 60 days.

Domain 2 — AI Risk Management (A.3): Built the risk assessment process for AI systems — using the classification methodology above. Each Tier 1 model had a full AI System Impact Assessment (ASIA). Tier 2 models had a standard risk assessment. Risk treatment plans for 8 high-risk models designed and assigned to model owners.

Domain 3 — AI System Life Cycle (A.6): Designed AI-specific gates in the SDLC — pre-deployment checklist: data quality assessment, bias testing, model performance benchmarks, security review, human oversight mechanism verification. No model moves to production without passing all gates. Embedded into the engineering CI/CD pipeline as a mandatory approval step.

Domain 4 — Data for AI Systems (A.7): Data quality requirements for training datasets — documented lineage, provenance, known limitations, bias flags. Implemented for all Tier 1 and Tier 2 models. Linked to the GDPR RoPA for models processing personal data.

Domain 5 — Information for Interested Parties (A.8): Transparency documentation — model cards for all Tier 1 models, published internally. External-facing AI features required UI disclosure ("This feature is AI-powered") per EU AI Act limited-risk transparency obligations.

Domain 6 — Human Oversight (A.9): Documented the oversight mechanism for each model: fully automated, human-in-the-loop (human approves before output acts), human-on-the-loop (human can override), or human-in-command (human initiates every action). All 8 High-Risk EU AI Act models upgraded to at minimum human-on-the-loop — no fully automated high-risk decisions.

Domain 7 — Supplier & Third-Party AI (A.10): Extended the model register to include third-party AI APIs — OpenAI, Google Gemini, Anthropic — used internally. Each third-party AI service was risk-assessed. Contracts reviewed for AI-specific provisions: data processing, model versioning notifications, output accuracy representations.
Q: What is a model card and why does it matter for governance?
A model card is a structured documentation artefact that describes an AI model's purpose, performance characteristics, limitations, and appropriate use cases. Originally proposed by Google researchers; now a standard transparency mechanism.

A good model card contains: model description and purpose; intended use cases and out-of-scope use cases; training data description (sources, size, known biases); performance metrics by demographic subgroup where applicable; known limitations and failure modes; human oversight mechanism; and contact/ownership information.

Why it matters for governance: It's the audit trail for "did you know what this model could and couldn't do when you deployed it?" Without a model card, a governance failure ("the model discriminated against group X") has no documented baseline. With a model card, you can show: we documented this limitation, we implemented this mitigation, we intended this oversight mechanism. That's the difference between negligence and managed risk.

For this client, we produced model cards for all 8 Tier 1 models. Stored in the model register. Reviewed and re-issued every 12 months or after any significant model update.
Q: How does ISO 42001 relate to the EU AI Act and NIST AI RMF?
They're three different instruments — a standard, a regulation, and a voluntary framework — that operate at different levels.

EU AI Act — binding EU law. Risk-tiered obligations. High-risk AI systems must meet specific requirements (conformity assessment, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity). It's prescriptive on what you must achieve; it doesn't tell you how to build the management system to achieve it. Applies to any company placing AI in the EU market — including non-EU firms.

ISO 42001 — certifiable international standard. Provides the management system architecture to systematically meet AI governance obligations — including EU AI Act requirements. Think of it as "the how" to the AI Act's "the what." Achieving ISO 42001 certification provides a defensible basis for EU AI Act conformance for the management system layer.

NIST AI RMF — voluntary US framework from NIST (2023). Four core functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. More conceptual and flexible than ISO 42001. Better as a thinking tool and maturity model. Some US enterprise customers ask their vendors to self-assess against NIST AI RMF — treat it like SOC 2 in spirit, not in audit structure.

How I used all three together: NIST AI RMF as the diagnostic — ran a current-state maturity assessment against all four functions. ISO 42001 as the programme architecture — structured the controls, documentation, and lifecycle management. EU AI Act as the regulatory obligations layer — mapped each high-risk model to specific EU AI Act articles and tracked conformance.
Q: What's the hardest part of AI governance in practice?
Getting data scientists and ML engineers to engage with governance as a discipline, not a bureaucratic overhead.

The resistance is real and understandable — most data scientists were hired to build models, not fill in risk registers. The programme succeeded because I made two design choices early:

One — embed, don't append. Instead of creating a separate AI governance process, we embedded the governance gates directly into the existing ML platform and CI/CD pipeline. The pre-deployment checklist appears in their tooling — it's not a separate form to email someone. Friction reduced from "file a ticket and wait 2 weeks" to "click, attest, deploy."

Two — make governance useful to engineers. The model card template we built wasn't just a compliance artefact — it became the standard handover document when models move between teams or when a data scientist leaves. Engineers started asking for model cards on third-party models they were evaluating. Governance became a tool, not a tax.

That mindset shift — from "governance is compliance overhead" to "governance is how we build trustworthy AI" — is the real programme outcome.
Q: How did you handle the "shadow AI" problem — models deployed without governance?
The 12 shadow models discovered during discovery were the first governance crisis of the programme. Three were in production, handling real customer data.

Immediate response: froze new AI deployments outside the register until the shadow inventory was cleared. Not a permanent freeze — a 3-week hold while we triaged. Informed the DPO because two of the shadow models processed personal data — potential GDPR Article 30 (RoPA) gap.

Root cause: no mandatory registration process. Engineers could spin up a SageMaker endpoint without any governance checkpoint. No automated detection.

Fix: three controls. One — IAM policy change: SageMaker, Azure ML, and Vertex AI endpoint creation now requires a tag with a valid Model Register ID. Without the tag, the deployment fails. Two — weekly cloud scanner: automated job scans all cloud environments for AI endpoints not in the register; alerts the AI Governance team. Three — culture: mandatory 30-minute AI governance onboarding for all new data science hires; existing data scientists completed it as part of the programme rollout.

After fix: zero unregistered deployments in the subsequent 6 months.

🎬 The Scene

PrecisionTech Engineering — a German-British precision manufacturer, €750M revenue, 4,500 employees, three manufacturing sites (Stuttgart, Coventry, Singapore). They supply machined components to Airbus, BMW, and Rolls-Royce. In March, Airbus procurement sent a formal notice: “All Tier-1 suppliers must hold ISO 27001 certification by Q1 next year or face supply chain review.” The contract at risk: €28M annually. Their CISO had never run an ISMS implementation. I was brought in as lead consultant.

The 60-second version

“I led ISO 27001 certification for a European precision manufacturer whose largest customer — Airbus — issued a supply chain ultimatum: certify in 18 months or lose a €28M contract. The challenge was unusual — this was a manufacturing environment, not a tech company. Their biggest security challenge was OT/IT convergence: shop floor PLCs feeding into corporate ERP. We scoped the ISMS across three sites, ran a 27005 risk assessment that surfaced 47 threats, selected 78 Annex A controls, and built the entire policy and evidence programme from scratch. We passed the Stage 2 audit with only two minor nonconformities — both closed in 14 days. Certified in 17 months, one month ahead of the Airbus deadline. The €28M contract was renewed, and two new OEM customers signed in the six months following certification, citing ISO 27001 as a deciding factor.”

The project phases — how to walk through it

Q: How did you approach scoping — that’s always the most debated step in ISO 27001.
Scoping is where most organisations fight and most consultants capitulate. I used three rules.

Rule 1: Follow the data, not the org chart. The Airbus contract required protecting design drawings, machining tolerances, and supplier data. We traced every system those assets touched: CAD workstations running CATIA, the SAP ERP, the supplier portal, and the file servers storing technical documentation. That defined the ISMS boundary.

Rule 2: Include the OT boundary explicitly. The shop floor PLCs talked to the MES (Manufacturing Execution System), which fed the ERP. Most manufacturers exclude OT from scope and pretend the air gap exists. It did not. We explicitly scoped OT/IT integration points and applied appropriate controls at the boundary.

Rule 3: Three sites, one ISMS. We fought to keep all three manufacturing sites in scope. A multi-site ISMS is harder to implement but the certification is far more credible — and Airbus specifically asked about global coverage. One scope statement, one ISMS boundary document, one certification body audit across all three sites.
Q: Walk me through your risk assessment approach under ISO 27005.
We used an asset-based, scenario-driven approach aligned to ISO 27005:2022.

Step 1 — Asset inventory. 340 information assets identified across three categories: information assets (design files, customer contracts, machining specs), software assets (CATIA, SAP, supplier portal, PLCs firmware), and physical assets (server rooms, OT cabinets). Each asset given a criticality score based on confidentiality, integrity, and availability impact to the business.

Step 2 — Threat scenarios. 47 threat scenarios identified. Top threats unique to this sector: IP theft by competitor intelligence services (especially for aerospace-grade tolerances), ransomware targeting OT/ERP integration points, supplier portal compromise leading to customer data exposure, and insider threats on the shop floor accessing CAD systems.

Step 3 — Risk evaluation. Likelihood × Impact matrix, 5×5 scale. 28 risks classified as HIGH or CRITICAL. 19 treated as MEDIUM with existing controls.

Step 4 — Risk treatment. Every HIGH/CRITICAL risk got a treatment option: Mitigate (most), Transfer (cyber insurance for 3 residual risks), Accept (2 risks approved by CISO with documented rationale). All captured in the risk register — the document the certification auditor scrutinises most.
Q: How did you build the Statement of Applicability?
The SoA is the centrepiece of ISO 27001 — it’s the formal document saying which of the 93 Annex A controls you apply and why, and which you exclude and why.

We selected 78 of 93 controls. The 15 excluded controls were genuinely not applicable — for example, A.5.14 (Information transfer policies for removable media) was excluded because the manufacturing environment had a hardware block on USB ports, making the policy redundant. Every exclusion had a written justification. That paper trail matters because auditors will challenge exclusions.

The SoA was built in three passes:
• Pass 1: Risk-driven selection — every HIGH risk drove at least one control selection.
• Pass 2: Legal/contractual requirements — Airbus’s supplier security requirements added 6 controls we might otherwise have deprioritised.
• Pass 3: Senior management review — CISO and Legal signed the final SoA. ISO 27001 requires documented evidence of that approval.

Result: 78 controls, 31 owned by IT, 12 owned by HR, 8 owned by Legal, 27 shared. Every control had a named owner, an implementation status, and a linked evidence item.
Q: What was the OT/IT convergence challenge specifically?
The PLCs on the shop floor ran proprietary firmware that had not been patched in 4 years. They communicated with the MES over a flat network — no segmentation between the shop floor and the corporate LAN. One compromised workstation in Stuttgart could theoretically reach a PLC in Singapore.

The fix was a network segmentation project running in parallel with our ISMS build. We implemented:
• Industrial DMZ between OT and IT networks
• Unidirectional security gateways (data diodes) at OT/IT boundary points
• Vendor remote access controls (OEM engineers accessing PLCs remotely had no MFA — fixed immediately)
• OT-specific asset inventory added to the ISMS scope

The auditor flagged this as a strength — most manufacturers either exclude OT from scope or have no documented OT security at all. We had explicit controls, a documented boundary, and evidence of segmentation testing.
Q: How did you prepare for the certification audit?
Two-phase preparation:

Stage 1 (Documentation review — Month 14). The certification body reviewed our ISMS documentation: scope, SoA, risk assessment, policies (22 policies written), and management review minutes. We received a Stage 1 report with 4 minor observations — three were wording issues in policies, one was an incomplete management review agenda. All addressed within 2 weeks.

Stage 2 (Controls testing — Months 15–17). The auditor selected 12 controls for deep testing — sampled across IT, HR, and OT domains. Evidence required for each: the control existed, it was operating, and it was effective over time. Two minor nonconformities raised: access review evidence for one subsidiary system was incomplete for one quarter, and the incident response plan had not been formally tested (tabletop exercise planned but not yet run). Both closed within 14 days. Certification awarded.

The secret weapon: Six months before Stage 2 I ran an internal mock audit — walked my team through the same 12 control areas using the certification body’s own audit checklists. Every gap we found internally was a finding we did not give to the external auditor.
Q: What was the hardest part of this project?
Culture, not technology. PrecisionTech had 4,500 employees with an engineering-first mindset. Security felt like bureaucracy to people who’d been machining aircraft parts for 30 years. The CISO had limited authority over the shop floor operations teams.

We solved it three ways. First, we tied security to the Airbus contract explicitly — every all-hands communication said “this certification is worth €28M. Every one of you is protecting that contract by completing security training.” Second, we made control owners heroes, not targets — the monthly control health dashboard named the best-performing control owner teams, not just the gaps. Third, the CISO used the certification milestone as a board-level agenda item — executive visibility meant managers took it seriously when their team’s controls were amber.
Q: What would you do differently?
Start the internal audit programme six months earlier. We trained three internal auditors in month 10 but didn’t run the first internal audit until month 13 — too close to Stage 2. Internal audits need time to generate findings, close them, and demonstrate the PDCA cycle working. Auditors look for evidence that the ISMS is self-correcting. We had two audit cycles; three would have been stronger.

Also: scope the policy suite earlier. Writing 22 policies in 4 months while simultaneously doing the risk assessment was a bottleneck. I would phase the policy writing to start in month 2, not month 6.

Compliance frameworks — deep dive

For each framework: what it is, when used, your story, common questions.

ISO 27001:2022

What it is: The international standard for information security management systems. Certifiable. The "spine" of most enterprise InfoSec programmes.

Architecture: Clauses 4-10 (management system requirements) + Annex A (93 controls in 4 themes: Organizational 37, People 8, Physical 14, Technological 34).

Anchor: "4-10 + 93/4" — Clauses 4 to 10 + 93 controls in 4 themes. If you remember these two numbers you can navigate the entire standard.
Q: Walk me through clauses 4-10.
4. Context — stakeholders, scope, boundaries.
5. Leadership — policy, roles, top-management commitment.
6. Planning — risk assessment methodology, risk treatment, Statement of Applicability, objectives.
7. Support — resources, competence, awareness, documents.
8. Operation — operational planning, risk assessment, risk treatment in practice.
9. Performance Evaluation — monitoring, internal audit, management review.
10. Improvement — non-conformity and corrective action.
All four management-system standards (27001, 9001, 14001, 45001) share clauses 4-10 — that's "Annex SL" or the High-Level Structure. Makes integration possible.
Q: What changed in ISO 27001:2022 versus 2013?
Three changes. One — Annex A restructured from 114 controls to 93. Same coverage, less duplication.
Two — controls reorganized into 4 themes (Organizational, People, Physical, Technological) instead of 14 categories. Easier mapping.
Three — 11 new controls added — most notable: threat intelligence (A.5.7), cloud security (A.5.23), ICT readiness for business continuity (A.5.30), data masking (A.8.11), information deletion (A.8.10), web filtering (A.8.23), secure coding (A.8.28), configuration management (A.8.9).
Existing certifications had to transition by 31 October 2025.
Q: What is the Statement of Applicability?
The SoA is the document that lists all 93 Annex A controls and marks each as Applicable or Not Applicable, with justification. The audit's centre of gravity — auditors spend more time on the SoA than any other document.
Every "Yes" needs evidence of operation. Every "No" needs strong justification (not "not relevant" — it must be specific). Common mistakes: outdated SoA, generic "Not Applicable" justifications, missing implementation status. Get the SoA right and the audit goes well.
Q: Stage 1 vs Stage 2 audit?
Stage 1 = documentation audit. "Are you ready on paper?" Mostly off-site, 3-5 days. Auditor reviews policies, SoA, risk register. Output: list of areas of concern + go/no-go for Stage 2.
Stage 2 = implementation audit. "Are you doing what you say?" On-site, 5-10 days. Auditor witnesses controls operating, interviews staff, samples evidence aggressively. Output: certification decision (with any non-conformities listed).
Anchor: Stage 1 = paper. Stage 2 = practice.

SOC 2 Type II

What it is: An AICPA attestation standard for service organizations. Auditor confirms controls operated effectively over a period (6-12 months). Customers use it to trust their vendors.

Five Trust Service Criteria: Security (mandatory), Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, Privacy.

Q: What's a "qualified opinion"?
An auditor can issue an unqualified (clean), qualified, adverse, or disclaimer opinion. Qualified means: the controls were operating effectively in most areas, but there's a specific identified weakness or exception. The qualification names the issue. Better than adverse but worse than clean — and customers will ask about it.
Q: What's the audit period?
Typically 6-12 months for SOC 2 Type II. The first Type II is often a 6-month period (you don't have a year of evidence yet). Subsequent annual cycles run 12 months. The auditor samples across the period — quarterly access reviews must show 4 cycles, monthly patching must show 12 cycles, etc.

PCI DSS v4.0

What it is: Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard. Required for anyone storing, processing, or transmitting cardholder data. v4.0 became fully effective March 2025.

Structure: 6 Goals → 12 Requirements → 250+ sub-requirements.

Q: What are the 6 goals of PCI DSS?
1. Build and maintain a secure network and systems
2. Protect cardholder data
3. Maintain a vulnerability management programme
4. Implement strong access control
5. Regularly monitor and test networks
6. Maintain an information security policy
Q: What's the biggest change in v4.0?
Two big themes. One — customized approach. v4.0 allows entities to design their own controls that meet a stated objective, instead of strictly prescriptive controls. More flexibility but more documentation.
Two — strengthened authentication: MFA on all access to cardholder data environment (CDE), not just admins. Stronger password requirements. Periodic risk-based re-authentication.

HIPAA Security Rule

What it is: US federal law protecting electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI). Required for covered entities and business associates.

Three safeguard categories: Administrative (workforce training, access management), Physical (facility access, workstation use), Technical (access control, audit logs, encryption).

Q: What's the difference between a covered entity and a business associate?
Covered entity = the healthcare provider, health plan, or clearinghouse that creates / receives PHI directly.
Business associate = a vendor processing PHI on behalf of a covered entity. SaaS hosting EHRs, cloud providers, claims processors. Business Associate Agreement (BAA) is mandatory.
Both are obligated under HIPAA but their liability and breach reporting paths differ.

NIST CSF 2.0

What it is: Voluntary US framework. Six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. The de facto US cyber baseline.

Q: Walk me through the 6 functions.
Govern — establish & monitor the cybersecurity strategy, policies, roles. New in 2.0.
Identify — understand assets, risks, governance context.
Protect — implement safeguards to limit impact of events.
Detect — identify cybersecurity events.
Respond — take action on detected events.
Recover — restore capabilities.
Each function decomposes into categories → subcategories → references. NIST CSF 2.0 has ~108 subcategories.

Common Control Framework (CCF)

What it is: A unified control library that maps one control to multiple regulatory frameworks. The architectural pattern behind "test once, attest many."

Q: Why bother with a CCF instead of running each framework separately?
Four reasons. One — cost: typically saves 30-50% on aggregate audit effort.
Two — consistency: one source of truth for control language, evidence, ownership.
Three — agility: when a new regulation arrives, you map it to existing controls in the CCF rather than building a new framework's worth of controls from scratch.
Four — auditor experience: auditors get one evidence repository to examine, not five.
Risk: poorly designed CCFs over-consolidate and miss framework-specific nuances. The art is consolidating where requirements truly align and preserving difference where they don't.

ITGC (IT General Controls)

What it is: The set of IT controls that underpin financial reporting integrity. Required under SOX for US listed companies.

Q: What's the relationship between ITGCs and SOX?
SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley) Section 404 requires management to attest to the effectiveness of internal controls over financial reporting (ICFR). ITGCs are the IT subset of ICFR. If your financial systems have weak ITGCs — bad change management, bad access controls — your financial statements can't be trusted. So external auditors test ITGCs as part of the financial audit.
Strong ITGCs = financial auditors can rely on application controls = cheaper audit. Weak ITGCs = auditors must do substantive testing of every financial transaction = expensive audit + restatement risk.

Cloud security — deep dive

AWS, Azure, GCP — what every GRC consultant must know.

The Shared Responsibility Model

The single most important concept in cloud security. The cloud provider is responsible for "security of the cloud" — physical data centers, hypervisor, foundational services. The customer is responsible for "security in the cloud" — configurations, identity, data, application security, encryption keys, who has access.

The classic mistake: Assuming AWS / Azure / GCP "handles security." They handle their half. The customer's half is bigger than most realize — and that's where 90%+ of cloud breaches happen.
Q: For a SaaS provider on AWS — what's in scope for their SOC 2 audit?
Their half of the shared responsibility — everything they configure and operate in AWS. Their IAM policies, VPC configurations, S3 bucket policies, Security Hub findings, CloudTrail logging, KMS key management, Config rules, application security.
AWS's half (physical security, hypervisor integrity, etc.) is NOT in scope for the SaaS provider's SOC 2 — they rely on AWS's own SOC 2 / ISO 27001 / FedRAMP certifications to cover that. This is called a "carve-out" approach.
That's why every SaaS SOC 2 report lists AWS as a sub-service organization with carved-out controls. Customers (user entities) need to understand both sides.

CSPM, CIEM, CWPP, CNAPP

The cloud security tooling alphabet. Know what each means.

  • CSPM — Cloud Security Posture Management. Continuous detection of misconfigurations. Wiz, Prisma Cloud, Lacework. Native: AWS Security Hub, Azure Defender for Cloud, GCP SCC.
  • CIEM — Cloud Infrastructure Entitlement Management. Identifies over-permissioned identities — humans and machines. Solves "who can do what" at scale.
  • CWPP — Cloud Workload Protection Platform. Runtime protection for VMs, containers, serverless. EDR for cloud workloads.
  • CNAPP — Cloud-Native Application Protection Platform. The convergence of CSPM + CIEM + CWPP + vulnerability scanning + IaC scanning into one platform.
Q: How do you assess multi-cloud governance?
Standardize the question, not the tools. Define a unified set of governance domains — identity, network, data protection, logging, change management, secrets, etc. For each domain, articulate the expected control (e.g., "all customer data encrypted at rest with customer-managed keys"). Then verify the control on each cloud using its native or third-party tooling.
The output is a single posture report across all three clouds — not three separate reports. That's the difference between cloud security theatre and real multi-cloud governance.

CIS Benchmarks

Center for Internet Security publishes hardening benchmarks for almost every platform — AWS, Azure, GCP, Kubernetes, Linux, Windows, Cisco, etc. CIS Foundations Benchmark for AWS, for example, is the de facto baseline for any AWS account.

Q: How do you use CIS Benchmarks in a real engagement?
Three uses. One — assessment baseline: when scoping a cloud governance review, CIS gives you a published, defensible list to assess against.
Two — automated checks: AWS Security Hub, Azure Defender, GCP SCC, and third-party CSPM tools all check CIS rules natively. Plug in, get a posture report.
Three — control framework anchor: CIS L1 / L2 maps cleanly to ISO 27001 Annex A, SOC 2 CC criteria, PCI DSS, HIPAA technical safeguards. So one CIS-aligned baseline scores you against multiple frameworks.
SECTION 13

AI governance — deep dive

The fastest-growing area in GRC. Where you stand out.

NIST AI RMF

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework, released 2023. Four core functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. Voluntary US framework; becoming the de facto baseline.

Q: Walk me through the NIST AI RMF functions.
Govern — cultivate a culture of risk management; policies, accountability, oversight structures across the AI lifecycle.
Map — context-setting: who, what, where the AI is used; risk categorization; impact analysis.
Measure — analyse, assess, benchmark, monitor AI risks. Metrics, evaluation, testing.
Manage — prioritise risks and act on them. Treatment decisions, ongoing monitoring, incident response.
Each function decomposes into categories and subcategories — similar feel to CSF.

EU AI Act

The world's first comprehensive AI law. Risk-tiered: Prohibited / High-risk / Limited-risk / Minimal-risk. High-risk systems face heavy obligations — conformity assessment, transparency, human oversight, robustness, post-market monitoring.

Q: What's a "high-risk" AI system under the EU AI Act?
Two categories. (1) AI as safety component of products covered by existing EU product safety legislation (medical devices, machinery, toys, etc.).
(2) AI used in specified high-risk areas: critical infrastructure, education/vocational training, employment/worker management, access to essential services (credit, insurance), law enforcement, migration/asylum/border control, administration of justice.
High-risk = full conformity assessment, registration in EU database, transparency obligations, human oversight, accuracy/robustness/cybersecurity requirements.

ISO/IEC 42001

The first certifiable AI management system standard. Plan-Do-Check-Act, like ISO 27001 but for AI. Specifies an AIMS (Artificial Intelligence Management System) — governance, risk, lifecycle controls, oversight. Pairs with ISO 27001 (info sec) and ISO 27701 (privacy).

Q: When would you recommend an enterprise pursue ISO 42001?
Three triggers. One — enterprise sells AI-powered products to enterprise customers who are asking for trust signals.
Two — EU AI Act high-risk operation: 42001 certification is a strong evidence base for conformity assessment.
Three — strategic differentiator: first-mover AI governance maturity, especially in regulated sectors (finance, healthcare, public sector).
I usually frame it: if you already have ISO 27001, 42001 is a smaller incremental lift than building everything from scratch.

The 8 categories of AI risk to know

  • Hallucination — model generates false outputs presented as true.
  • Bias & fairness — outputs disadvantage protected classes.
  • Privacy — model memorises and leaks training data.
  • Prompt injection / jailbreak — adversarial inputs override safety controls.
  • Data poisoning — adversarial training data manipulates model behavior.
  • Model theft / extraction — model weights or capabilities extracted via API.
  • Excessive agency — autonomous AI takes actions beyond authorization.
  • Shadow AI — employees using AI tools the org doesn't know about.
Q: How do you build an AI inventory at a Fortune-500?
Four steps. One — survey: structured intake from product, engineering, data science, marketing teams. Use a standard AI definition.
Two — automated discovery: SaaS spend analysis, network traffic patterns, code repository scans (look for API calls to OpenAI, Anthropic, Bedrock, Azure OpenAI).
Three — risk tier each entry: low (internal-only, low-stakes) → high (customer-facing, high-stakes decisions).
Four — operating model: AI Governance Committee owns the inventory; standard updates quarterly; new AI use cases require pre-launch review.
Most enterprises discover 3-5x more AI than they thought. Inventory is the foundation of all AI governance.

SECTION 16

Consulting craft — Big 4 fluency

The unwritten rules that separate Big-4-grade consultants from technical specialists.

The 10 rules of senior consulting presence

  1. Pre-wire everything. Never surprise an executive in front of their board. Every finding in your deck should have been previewed with the affected exec privately first.
  2. Bring the answer, not the analysis. Senior people don't want your methodology — they want your conclusion. Methodology lives in the appendix.
  3. One slide, one message. If a slide has 4 messages, it has zero.
  4. Speak in their currency. CFO speaks dollars. CISO speaks risk. CEO speaks narrative. Same content; different framing.
  5. Calibrate certainty. "Highly confident" or "working hypothesis" or "we don't know yet." Never blur them.
  6. Pair every weakness with a path. "Your second line is captured" is a problem statement. "Your second line is captured; here's how we'd unwind it over 12 months for $X" is a recommendation.
  7. Protect your team. If an associate's work is challenged in the room, you take the hit and address in private.
  8. Never sell during delivery. Partners sell. Managers deliver. Confusing the two breaks both.
  9. Use silence. When the client says something important, count to three before responding. Don't fill space.
  10. End every meeting with one decision. Workshops that end without a decision are workshops that need to happen again.

How to facilitate a workshop (and look senior doing it)

  1. Open with the frame, not the questions. Spend the first 90 seconds on what you're trying to achieve, ground rules, time-box. Then start asking.
  2. Use real cases, not abstractions. "Walk me through what happens when X event occurs" beats "tell me about your incident response."
  3. Two notetakers. One for content; one for sentiment (body language, defensiveness, alliances). Sentiment notes go to the manager only.
  4. Probe with 5 'whys.' When an answer is vague, keep going: "Why is that?" → "Why?" → "Why?" → until you hit the actual cause.
  5. Capture dissent. When two execs disagree, write down both views verbatim. Dissent is data.
  6. End with one decision + one ask. "We've agreed X. Owner is Y. Date is Z. I need [evidence/access/intro] from you by [date]."

How to handle a hostile question in a workshop

  • Don't react instantly. Pause. Three seconds.
  • Acknowledge first, defend second. "I hear you — let me address that directly."
  • Reframe if needed. "What I think you're really asking is X — is that right?"
  • Answer with evidence, not assertion. "Here's the specific data point we observed: [X]."
  • If you're wrong, say so. "You're right — let me revise that. The correct picture is…"
  • If they're wrong, hold the line politely. "I understand that view; here's why I'd still recommend X based on the evidence."
